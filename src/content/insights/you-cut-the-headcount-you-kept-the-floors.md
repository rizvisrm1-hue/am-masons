_Why the cheapest time to plan for change is before you move in_

By Rashid Masood Rizvi, Founder, AM Masons Advisory

Last week, Canva cut 22% of its staff, 1,271 people. Microsoft has offered voluntary exits to around 60,000 employees. Through October, US employers announced more than 617,000 job cuts this year. About one in five cited AI; most of the rest pointed to slowing growth, market conditions and restructuring. The announcements all read the same way: leaner teams, lower costs, sharper focus.

Almost none of them mention the office.

Canva is a useful exception. In the same announcement, it told hybrid staff to be in the office two to three days a week from January. That is two real estate questions in one email: how much space, and how often it gets used.

## The cost nobody modelled

When a company cuts 20% of its people, the payroll saving shows up next quarter. The real estate does not move. The lease was signed for the headcount the company had, or expected to have, three or five years ago. Rent, operating costs, cleaning, security, utilities and insurance carry on exactly as before.

Take an illustrative company of 400 people in roughly 70,000 square feet downtown. It cuts 80 roles. On paper, about 14,000 square feet is now surplus. At $60 a square foot in gross rent, that is $840,000 a year. With five years left on the lease, the company is carrying more than $4 million for space nobody will sit in. That number rarely makes it into the restructuring deck.

Public filings give a sense of scale. In 2023, as Meta cut staff and shrank its office footprint, it reported about US$2.5 billion in facilities consolidation charges, more than double its severance costs. Alphabet reported US$1.85 billion in office space charges next to US$2.09 billion in severance. This is not just a big tech problem: when Lyft cut staff in late 2022, its real estate charges were more than twice its severance. Not every dollar of those charges is rent. Some covers writing off fit-outs, and some covers reworking the space that stays. But it is fair to assume a significant share is the cost of floors nobody uses. And that cost lasts. Salesforce finished its people actions within a year but expected its office exits to run into fiscal 2026. Severance is paid once. Surplus space is paid every month until the lease ends, or until someone pays to end it. Closer to home, Shopify listed more than 348,000 square feet at The Well in Toronto for sublease in early 2023.

## Three doors, and most are locked

A company in this position has three options.

The first is to hand space back, if the lease includes a contraction right. Few do, and the ones that exist come with conditions and a fee.

The second is to sublease. That depends on what the lease says about subletting, and on whether the surplus space can actually be let to someone else.

The third is to negotiate with the landlord. That works when the landlord needs something from you, such as a longer term, or wants your space for another tenant. In a tightening market, landlords need less from you than they did two years ago.

## Why the doors are locked

Here is the part most companies only discover when they need to change course. The contract is half the problem. The layout is the other half.

Most offices are designed as one continuous floor plate. Meeting rooms sit in the middle. The kitchen, the IT room and reception serve the whole floor. Teams are placed next to the people they work with, which makes sense on day one but spreads them across the whole floor. When it is time to give back 14,000 square feet, there is no clean 14,000 square feet to give. Every option cuts through something shared.

So the company is left with two bad choices. Carry the whole space for the rest of the term, or sublease all of it and move. The first wastes money. The second is a relocation nobody planned, in the middle of a restructuring.

Carrying the space has a cost that never shows up in the ledger. Walk into an office after a layoff. Rows of empty desks. Nameplates still on some of them. The people who stayed see the gaps every day. Research on layoff survivors has documented this for decades; in one 2008 survey of more than 4,000 survivors, 74% said their own productivity had fallen. A half-empty floor does not let anyone move on. It keeps the cut on display.

## Core and ancillary

The answer is to design for optionality before you move in. The method I use with clients splits every office into two parts: core and ancillary.

Core is not a headcount number. It is the set of services the office cannot run without: reception, the IT room, the cafeteria (if you have one) and purpose-built spaces such as an innovation lab. These are expensive to build and disruptive to move. You put them together in one zone, build them once, and plan to keep them for the full term.

Ancillary is everything else: the workstations, team areas, meeting rooms and focus rooms people use every day. It is built to exactly the same standard as the core. Ancillary does not mean cheaper, and it does not mean extra space you hope to grow into. What makes it ancillary is that it holds no core service, so it can be added, released or moved without touching the rest of the office. In practice that means planning it in clean, separable blocks, ideally a full floor or a wing with its own access to exits and washrooms, and backing it with the right contract terms: expansion and contraction rights, staggered terms, clear sublet rights.

That design works in every direction. When the business grows, you take the next block or floor and plug it into the same core. When it wins a contract, acquires a team or wants to move closer to its talent, the decision is about the space, not about unpicking a floor plate. And when it contracts, you release a block or sublet it cleanly while the core keeps running. Nobody spends a year walking past an empty row of desks.

## What optionality costs

None of this is free. Landlords price flexibility. A contraction right typically carries a fee tied to the unamortized cost of improvements and commissions. A separable floor may cost more to demise. Shorter terms or a right to terminate often mean slightly higher rent or a smaller improvement allowance.

Think of it as insurance. You pay a premium every year, and in most years you wonder why. Then the year comes when you need it, and the premium looks cheap next to the alternative.

Not every company needs the same cover. A business with stable headcount and a long planning horizon may rightly commit fully and take the better rate. A business exposed to automation, acquisition or a volatile market is better served by building optionality in. The point is to make that choice deliberately, before the lease is signed, while you still have the leverage to ask for it.

The restructuring plan will always find the payroll savings. The question is whether your real estate lets you keep them.
