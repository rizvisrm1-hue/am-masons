_Why real estate belongs in the first 100 days of an integration, not the second year_

By Rashid Masood Rizvi, Founder, AM Masons Advisory

Deals are back. In the first half of 2026, 708 Canadian companies were sold, more than in the same period last year. Each of those deals was priced on what the target brings: customers, product, talent, market share. The accounts were reviewed line by line.

The leases usually got a schedule. Rarely a plan.

## The number that told you very little

For years, rent sat in the income statement as an operating expense. It told a buyer what the company paid last year. It said nothing about what the company had committed to pay for the next ten.

IFRS 16 changed that for companies that report under it. Since 2019, leases have shown up on the balance sheet as a liability. That is progress, but it is not the whole picture. Many of the mid-size companies being bought in Canada are private and report under ASPE, which never adopted that approach. Their rent still sits in the income statement, as it always did. And even where the liability is on the balance sheet, it tells you how much. It does not tell you when each lease ends, what notice you must give, whether you can sublet, what it costs to restore the space, or whether any of it can be given back.

That is what the integration team needs to know. It is rarely what the deal team asked for.

## What you actually own now

The day after closing, the combined company has a bigger portfolio than either side planned for. Two offices in the same city, sometimes in the same district. Leases that end in different years. Two sets of workplace standards, two security systems, two ways of booking a meeting room. Space sized for a headcount that the integration plan may already have changed.

None of it is urgent on day one. Most of it becomes urgent by month nine.

## Too slow, too fast

There are two ways to get this wrong.

Leave both portfolios running separately for too long and the deal stays an acquisition, not a merger. People keep their old badges, their old floors and their old loyalties. The new company exists on the org chart and nowhere else. And you pay for two of everything while you wait.

Force everyone together too quickly and you create a different problem. Teams move into space that was never designed for them. Leases get broken at whatever price the landlord names. And the people the deal was meant to keep start to wonder whether they want to stay.

The right pace depends on the portfolio. You cannot pick the pace until you can see the whole portfolio.

## Start with one view

The first step is a lease obligation tracker for the combined company. Every lease, on both sides, in one place: address, size, headcount, rent, expiry, notice dates, renewal and termination options, sublet rights and restoration obligations.

It is not glamorous work. It is the work that makes every later decision faster, cheaper and easier to defend. It turns two filing cabinets into one picture of what the business owns, what it owes and where it has room to move.

## Then deal with the next 12 months

With the tracker in hand, look first at every lease that expires or needs notice in the next 12 months. Those decisions are already on the clock, and the landlord knows it.

For each one, decide what the combined business actually needs from that location. Keep it, shrink it, merge it into another site, or let it go. Then approach the landlord with a position, not a question. A tenant that arrives knowing what it wants negotiates very differently from one that arrives asking for options.

Once the near-term leases are settled, the longer runway opens up. That is where the bigger moves sit: consolidating sites, bringing teams under one roof, and building in the optionality the new company will need.

## Say it out loud

The last piece is the one most often skipped. Announce the strategy.

People on both sides of a deal want to know where they will work and when that might change. Silence does not make that question go away. It fills with rumour. A clear, published plan, even one that says some decisions are still twelve months away, tells people the leadership team has looked at the whole picture and is making choices on purpose.
