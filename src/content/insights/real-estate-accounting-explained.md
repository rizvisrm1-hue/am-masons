_What IFRS 16 changed, what it didn't, and what every CEO and CFO still needs to know about their leases_

By Rashid Masood Rizvi, Founder, AM Masons Advisory

I enjoy almost everything about corporate real estate and workplace. Finding the right building. Designing space around how people actually work. Watching a team settle into an office that fits them.

The one part I have never enjoyed is the accounting.

## Owning looked worse than it was

For years, a financial statement told a strange story about real estate. Buy a building and it sat on your balance sheet as an asset, with any mortgage as debt and depreciation running through the income statement every year. Lease the same building and the commitment barely showed up. Rent went through as an expense. What you owed for the next ten years sat in a note at the back.

On paper, the tenant looked leaner, carried less debt and earned a better return on its assets. The owner looked heavier, even when owning was the cheaper long-term decision. Anyone comparing the two on the statements alone was being nudged toward leasing.

## The same lease, different numbers

Then there is the judgement involved. A lease does not have one value. It depends on what the accountant assumes.

How long is the term? If there is a right to extend, will you use it? If there is an early exit, will you take it? Each answer changes the number. Two finance teams can read the same lease, make different calls on the options, and report different figures. Neither is wrong. But a reader of the accounts would never know which calls were made.

## Then came IFRS 16

IFRS 16 came into force in 2019 and moved leases onto the balance sheet. A company now records the right to use the space as an asset and its future rent as a liability, a lot like a loan. The asset is depreciated evenly. The liability carries interest, which is highest in the early years and falls as the balance is paid down.

I am still not sure whether it helped or hurt. Here is why, using an illustrative 10-year lease at $1 million a year.

| | Before IFRS 16 | After IFRS 16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash paid each year | $1.000M | $1.000M |
| Expense in year 1 | $1.000M rent | $1.178M ($0.736M depreciation + $0.442M interest) |
| Expense in year 10 | $1.000M rent | $0.793M ($0.736M depreciation + $0.057M interest) |
| Total expense over 10 years | $10.0M | $10.0M |
| On the balance sheet on day 1 | Nothing | $7.36M asset and $7.36M liability |
| Effect on EBITDA | Rent reduces EBITDA by $1.0M a year | None: rent no longer sits above EBITDA |

The cash did not change. The total cost did not change. But EBITDA improved by the full value of the rent, because rent moved below the line as depreciation and interest. Debt went up by the value of the lease. And the early years of every new lease started to look more expensive than they really are.

The case for IFRS 16 is visibility. A ten-year commitment can no longer hide in a footnote, and leasing and owning now look more alike on the balance sheet. The case against is that it made some numbers look better and others look worse without changing anything about the business. A flattering EBITDA, a heavier debt load and a relocation that looks expensive in year one can all push decisions in the wrong direction if nobody looks underneath them.

## Canada adds another layer

In Canada, not everyone follows IFRS 16. Public companies do. Many private companies report under ASPE, the standards for private enterprises, which kept the old approach: rent as an expense, commitments in the notes. The standard-setter is reviewing that section, but for now nothing has changed. Companies that report under US rules use ASC 842, which puts leases on the balance sheet but, for most office leases, still shows a single rent expense.

So the same lease can look three different ways depending on who signed it. Comparing yourself with a peer, or with a company you are about to buy, means knowing which rulebook each set of numbers follows.

## The accounts are not the portfolio

Knowing how your real estate is accounted for matters for every CEO and CFO. It shapes how lenders, boards and buyers read the business.

Equally important is knowing the obligations across your entire portfolio, lease by lease. The accounts tell you how much you owe. They do not tell you when each lease ends, what notice you must give and by when, whether you can sublet or give space back, what it will cost to restore the space when you leave, or whether the space still fits the business.

That information lives in a lease tracker, not in the financial statements. It is what you need on the day a lease decision comes up.

_The 10-year lease example is illustrative: $1 million a year, paid at year end, 6% discount rate, straight-line depreciation. This article is a general explanation, not accounting advice; speak to your auditor about how the standards apply to your leases._
