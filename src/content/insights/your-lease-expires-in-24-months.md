_Why the time to plan an office lease decision is long before the renewal letter arrives_

By Rashid Masood Rizvi, Founder, AM Masons Advisory

I have sat across the table from a lot of leadership teams this year talking about their real estate. The same pattern keeps showing up. Few know how their space is actually being used. Even fewer know when their lease ends.

That is not a criticism. In a company of 500 or 1,500 people, real estate is rarely anyone's full-time job. It sits somewhere between finance, HR and the office manager, and it only becomes urgent when something forces it. For most companies, that something is a letter from the landlord.

## The renewal letter trap

The renewal notice arrives. Someone forwards it to the CFO. The more organized companies call their broker about six months before expiry and start negotiating. On paper, that looks responsible. In practice, the options are already gone. A relocation takes far longer than six months once you account for the search, design, approvals, construction and furniture lead times. Six months out, you cannot credibly move, and the landlord knows it. Even the best broker cannot create leverage that expired a year ago. You end up negotiating the size of the discount on a decision that was made for you.

For the last few years, that mistake was forgivable. The market was soft. Sublease space was everywhere, landlords were anxious, and tenants who showed up late still walked away with free rent and generous improvement allowances.

That market is gone.

## The squeeze is real

Trophy office vacancy in Canada is at a six-year low, with Toronto and Montreal at 3% or below. Class A availability in Toronto fell to 10.4% in the third quarter. Industry research now shows negotiating power has shifted toward owners. The same thing is happening south of the border, where Manhattan trophy vacancy just hit a cycle low of 4.9%.

Return-to-office mandates are adding fuel. When several of Canada's largest banks pushed to four days a week, employees ended up hunting for desks and leadership went looking for more space. Mandates get announced first. The math on whether the floors can hold everyone comes later. Every company that does this is now competing for the same shrinking pool of quality buildings, and the gap between the best and the rest has never been wider. Downtown Class A vacancy nationally sits at 13.8%. Class B and C sits at 23.8%.

## When to start

Eighteen months before expiry is the minimum. The bigger your footprint, the earlier you begin. Under 20,000 square feet, start 18 to 24 months out. Between 20,000 and 50,000, give yourself 24 to 36 months. Above 50,000, or for any decision that touches more than one site, you need 36 to 48 months. In this market, treat those numbers as the latest you should begin, not the comfortable window.

Starting early is not about touring buildings sooner. It is about answering two questions before someone else frames them for you. What do you actually need? And what can you give up?

## What you need, and what you can give up

Start with square feet. If people are in three days a week, are you paying for five? Most companies have never measured utilization properly. Badge data tells you who walked through the door. It does not tell you where they sat, which rooms sat empty, or which spaces quietly failed the people using them. And before you cut, know where your own mandate is heading. A footprint sized for two days a week will not survive a leadership announcement of four.

Then the address. Here is the question few ask out loud: what is the return on Class A? For some companies, the premium earns its keep. If you are recruiting in a tight talent market, hosting clients, or asking people to give up their commute, the building is part of the argument. For others, it is a line item that buys a nicer lobby. Fit-out costs in Toronto alone can run $200 to $300 a square foot, so whatever you choose, you will live with it for a decade or more.

And not every good building is downtown. Toronto's suburban vacancy sits around 21%. There are well-run Class A buildings outside the core with strong transit access, and tenants there still hold leverage that downtown tenants have lost. For a mid-size firm that needs a good building rather than a trophy address, the best deal in the market may be where few are looking.

## The lease is not the decision

The lease is the paperwork that follows the decision. The real decision is what your business needs from its space for the next ten to fifteen years: how many people, how often, doing what kind of work, and where they are willing to come. Answer that first and the negotiation gets simpler, faster and cheaper.

Wait for the renewal letter, and someone else will answer it for you.
